## How to use this guide

Work in small cycles: **understand a person → choose a useful outcome → make a clear offer → share it in the right place → learn from evidence → improve**. Keep a notebook or a simple spreadsheet. For every exercise, write down your assumptions and what you learned. Do not put customer passwords, payment information or private records into public AI tools.

## 1. Begin with a person and a problem

Marketing is not simply posting often. It is helping the right people understand a relevant offer and take a useful next step. Start with a real audience rather than “everyone.”

### A one-page audience brief

1. **Who is this for?** Describe a role, community or situation—not a stereotype.
2. **What are they trying to achieve?** Use their language where you can.
3. **What gets in the way?** Separate facts you know from guesses.
4. **What would a useful outcome look like?** Make it observable.
5. **Where do they already seek help or information?** Choose channels based on evidence.

Interview a few people respectfully. Ask open questions such as “What is the hardest part of…?” and “What have you already tried?” Do not treat a small conversation as proof of demand; use it to improve your questions and test your assumptions.

### Exercise

Choose a business or community project. Write a three-sentence audience brief, then list two things you need to verify before recommending a marketing campaign.

## 2. Shape an offer people can understand

A clear offer explains who it helps, what it helps them do and how to take the next step. A useful starter format is:

> For **[specific audience]** who want **[valuable outcome]**, **[service or product]** helps by **[plain-language benefit]**. The next step is **[one action]**.

Avoid unsupported claims such as “guaranteed sales,” “number one,” or “instant income.” State what is included, what is not included, how long the work takes, how many revisions apply and which outside costs may be separate.

### Exercise

Write two versions of one offer: a short headline and a two-sentence explanation. Ask someone in the intended audience what they think they would receive. Revise unclear words.

## 3. Map the customer journey

A simple journey might be:

- **Discovery:** a person sees a helpful post, referral or search result.
- **Consideration:** they compare options, review examples and ask questions.
- **Action:** they enquire, book, register or purchase.
- **Delivery:** the promised work or service is provided.
- **Follow-up:** the business checks satisfaction and offers a relevant next step.

At every step, ask what the person needs, what information the business needs, and how privacy and consent are respected. A shorter journey is not automatically better if it hides important terms or pressure-sells someone.

## 4. Plan content around value

Choose one objective for a content cycle: teach, explain an offer, answer a common question, show a process or invite an enquiry. A weekly plan can use three content types:

1. **Useful knowledge:** a short answer, checklist or demonstration.
2. **Evidence:** an original example, process or lesson learned—with permission.
3. **Invitation:** a clear, low-pressure call to action.

Keep content accessible: plain language, captions for video, readable contrast, descriptive links and alternative text for meaningful images. Check names, prices, dates and factual claims before publishing. Get consent before sharing a customer's work, image or story.

### Exercise

Create a seven-day content plan for one audience and one business goal. For each item, state the audience question it answers and the next step offered.

## 5. Select channels and measure what matters

Choose channels based on the audience and the task. A website can provide a stable source of information; social platforms can help with discovery and conversation; email can support permission-based follow-up. Each channel has different costs, rules and limits.

Use a simple measurement chain:

- **Attention:** relevant reach, visits or message opens.
- **Interest:** meaningful clicks, replies, saves or time with useful material.
- **Action:** qualified enquiries, bookings, registrations or sales.
- **Quality:** delivery, repeat use, satisfaction and useful feedback.

A number without context is not insight. Compare like with like, record the period and sample size, and note other changes that may explain a result. Never promise that a campaign will reach a particular ranking or revenue level.

## 6. Use AI as a helper—not a substitute for judgement

Generative AI can help brainstorm, outline, summarise approved material or produce a first draft. It can also invent details, repeat bias, expose confidential information or produce text that sounds certain while being wrong.

A responsible review routine:

1. Give the tool a bounded task and only the information it needs.
2. Ask it to mark assumptions and unknowns.
3. Verify claims, names, figures, links and advice against trustworthy sources.
4. Edit for the real audience, local context, accessibility and brand voice.
5. Obtain human approval before anything consequential is sent or published.

Do not paste personal data, client secrets, account credentials or sensitive business records into a tool unless the data owner has approved the use and the service is suitable.

## 7. Design an agentic workflow carefully

An AI agent or agentic workflow can use tools and carry out multi-step tasks. Before connecting one, document:

- **Purpose:** one specific task and an acceptable outcome.
- **Inputs:** approved sources and data minimisation.
- **Permissions:** the least access needed; prefer read-only at first.
- **Limits:** actions the agent must never take.
- **Review:** where a person checks a response or approves an action.
- **Escalation:** what happens when the request is unclear, risky or outside scope.
- **Monitoring:** how errors are noticed, reported and corrected.

Start with a supervised pilot, test ordinary and edge cases, and keep a human fallback. Do not let an untested agent publish, spend money, make eligibility decisions or handle sensitive matters without explicit safeguards and accountable human oversight.

### Exercise

Sketch a customer enquiry assistant. Identify three approved FAQs, two reasons it must hand off to a person, and one piece of data it should not collect.

## 8. Build a portfolio and keep learning

Save original, non-confidential examples: a campaign brief, a sample content calendar, a measurement snapshot or a workflow diagram. Explain the problem, your role, your method and what you learned. Ask permission before including real client material. Label practice concepts clearly so no one mistakes them for commissioned work.
